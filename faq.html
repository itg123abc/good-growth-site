Choosing your route

What is Good Growth building?

We’re developing a system for producing microbial inputs at a farm or through a local agricultural business. A mesh starter cartridge contains a bacterial culture within a proprietary blend of growth media. It is used in a reusable reactor, with an included carbon-source pack added partway through the cycle to feed continued growth.

The reactor is reused, and each new cycle starts with a fresh cartridge. Growers could operate a reactor, share production locally, or purchase from a future local producer.

How can I get involved?

Tell us a little about yourself, what you grow or do, and what caught your interest. You might want to try the inputs, produce at your farm, purchase from a future local producer, or help with testing. We’d like to hear from potential production and distribution partners too. Good Growth is still in development, so you don’t need to arrive with a proposal or know exactly where you fit. A question or an idea is enough to start.

Do I need my own reactor?

No. Some growers may want to produce at their own farm, while others would rather buy what they need from a nearby retailer, co-op, or local producer. We’re developing the system with both options in mind. If you prefer purchasing locally, you wouldn’t be responsible for running the production equipment. We’re still identifying potential local producers, so telling us where you’re located helps us understand where there’s interest.

Can I get involved if I have a small farm?

Yes. You can register interest in purchasing from a future local producer without operating equipment yourself. You could also explore producing for a small group of nearby growers, with one site hosting the reactor and an agreed operator looking after it.

Farm size alone does not decide the best route. We would discuss your production needs, application schedule, and shared arrangements. Local purchasing availability is still being developed.

Register purchasing interest or discuss shared production.

Which crops and operations can participate?

We’re open to hearing from growers across all crops and types of operations. Part of this work is finding out where the system can be most helpful and which applications are worth testing first. Tell us what you grow, where you’re located, and how you’d like to use the inputs through our contact form.

Operating the system

What would I need to do to run the Good Growth reactor?

You or someone on your team would look after the system. Current estimates are about two hours for initial setup and 5–10 minutes a day for routine checks and tasks, plus cleaning between cycles. Site integration can affect setup time, and cleaning time is still being established.

The work includes checking the run and adding the included carbon-source pack partway through. We would review your setup, operator responsibilities, and support needs together before a trial.

What space, water, and power would I need?

The current prototype uses a 55-gallon HDPE barrel, roughly 23¼ inches across and 36 inches tall. Separately, allow approximately 2 ft × 3 ft × 2 ft for the control box and pump, plus room to access and service both.

It operates at approximately 50 gallons of working volume, with access to municipal water and a standard wall outlet. A GFCI-protected cord is included. We would review water preparation and the site arrangement with you before use.

Can I use Good Growth with my existing irrigation or spraying equipment?

The design goal is flexibility across farm setups. Depending on the site, the liquid could be fed into a larger irrigation system using a suitable pump and hose, or transferred into mixing tanks for smaller, modular, or mobile systems. We’re also exploring compatible spraying methods. We would review filtration, delivery, handling, and application requirements with you before a trial; compatibility is still being evaluated.

How long does a production cycle take?

We’re currently planning around two weeks per cycle, with testing ahead to narrow down the best production endpoint and application timing. Early reactor observations suggest there may be some flexibility in when the output can be used, which we’re working to confirm through biological measurements. The goal is to give growers room to plan applications around their operation while maintaining the quality of what they apply.

Pilot costs and participation

What would taking part in a pilot look like?

It starts with a discussion about your operation and what you’d like to try. That might be comparing the inputs with your usual practice or finding out what running a Good Growth reactor at your farm would involve. Together, we’d work out what to test, what to record, when to do it, and who handles each task. We’d also discuss costs before you commit. Your feedback on anything that adds work, causes confusion, or could be simpler would help us improve the system.

Who pays for equipment, inputs, and testing?

We expect the first few trials to involve little or no cost to the participating farm. The reactor would be loaned for the trial rather than transferred into your ownership. We would agree on any costs and responsibilities before you commit.

You or your team would handle most day-to-day work. Ian would help with setup and integration and be available for troubleshooting and support. Good Growth expects to cover the agreed project-specific testing for these first trials; routine soil tests your farm already undertakes would normally remain with the farm.

Testing and quality

What are you hoping to learn from the pilots?

We want to find out whether the system makes sense on a real operation. That includes how the inputs perform, how they move through application equipment, how much work production takes, and what it costs. A trial might also look at plant establishment or root growth, with a comparison agreed beforehand. We haven’t established those benefits yet. The results—and your experience using the Good Growth reactor—will help us decide what works, what needs changing, and what to test next.

How will you check the quality of what’s produced?

We need to check which microbes are present, how many are alive, whether there’s contamination, and how consistent the output is from one batch to the next. We also need to understand what happens during handling and application. Those checks are part of the testing we’re developing, including the standards a batch would need to meet. Appearance or visible activity alone can’t tell us whether the output is suitable to use.
