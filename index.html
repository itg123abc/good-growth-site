Pilot-stage agricultural technology

Fresh biological inputs. Produced closer to use.

Good Growth is developing a reusable reactor and starter-cartridge system for producing agricultural microbial inputs near the farms and local partners that use them. We are exploring practical pilot opportunities to evaluate the system in real operating conditions.

Why local production

Why move production closer to application?

Biology is sensitive to the journey. Storage, handling, and application matter; local production is one approach to evaluating that journey.

Compact shipping

The model starts with compact inputs rather than relying entirely on transporting finished liquid.

Production timing

Local production can bring the production schedule closer to the intended application window.

Local access

A grower, retailer, or nearby operator could support production, depending on the deployment model.

The practical question is how this model compares on access, timing, operator effort, and total cost. We will evaluate those tradeoffs alongside biological performance and alternatives such as dry or stabilized products.

How it works

A cartridge, a reusable reactor, and a local point of production.

Our system is designed to keep local production practical: begin with a compact cartridge, grow it in a reusable reactor, then apply the output through methods that fit real farm operations.

1. Start with a compact cartridge

Compact starter cartridges provide the inputs for each production cycle, including the mid-cycle growth inputs. The reusable reactor supports recurring production.

2. Grow in a reusable reactor

The process uses a reusable reactor and mid-cycle growth inputs near a grower or local partner. Local production does not have to mean every grower owns or operates a reactor.

3. Plan application around the site

The design goal is integration with the irrigation equipment growers already use. Pilots will help us understand what connects well and how to improve the fit, while also exploring compatible spraying methods.

Two paths to local production

Closer to use can take more than one shape.

Not every grower needs to own a reactor. We are exploring two ways to bring production closer to the people who use the output.

At a grower's site

Produce where you grow.

Recurring cartridges→On-site reactor→Application

For growers interested in taking part in production. Pilot planning would explore the setup, operator tasks, and how production fits the application schedule.

Through a nearby partner

Local access. Shared capability.

Recurring cartridges→Partner reactor→Nearby growers

For retailers, co-ops, and operators interested in producing near the growers they serve. This route explores access to output without every grower managing equipment.

Conceptual deployment paths. Local partner availability and operating arrangements are part of the development work.

Development

Building the evidence alongside the system.

Good Growth has early prototype experience. The next stage brings biological evaluation and practical pilot planning together: understanding what the system produces, how it operates, and where it can provide useful value.

Our evaluation priorities include output quality and consistency, irrigation and spraying integration, operator effort, and the full cost of local production. Controlled efficacy results are not yet established; these questions guide the work ahead.

Any future public findings will identify the conditions, comparisons, and limitations of the work.

Have another question? Let's talk →
What is Good Growth developing?

Good Growth is developing a system for producing agricultural microbial inputs closer to where they are used. It combines a reusable reactor with compact starter cartridges and included mid-cycle growth inputs. Production could take place at a farm or with a nearby agricultural partner. The goal is to make local biological production practical while evaluating the output, day-to-day workload, and economics together. The reactor enables repeated production cycles; recurring cartridges provide the inputs for each cycle.

How can I get involved?

Start with a conversation about what you grow, how you currently use biological inputs, and what you would want a better approach to make possible. We are interested in growers who could help evaluate an application, partners exploring local production, and collaborators who can contribute to testing or practical deployment. The system is still in development, so the next step is to explore a useful pilot opportunity rather than place a standard product order.

Do I need to own or operate a reactor?

Not necessarily. We are exploring both production at a grower's site and production through a nearby retailer, co-op, or other agricultural partner. Some growers may want direct involvement in production; others may prefer access to locally produced inputs without managing equipment. These are two different ways to bring production closer to use. We can talk through which route interests you and the responsibilities that would come with it.

Who would operate the equipment?

The operator could be a grower or a local production partner. The practical work includes preparing the system, monitoring the run, adding the mid-cycle inputs, and preparing for the next cycle. An important part of development is understanding the time, instructions, and support needed to do those tasks reliably. Before a pilot begins, we would identify who handles each task and discuss training, support, and equipment responsibilities together.

Can the system integrate with my irrigation or spraying equipment?

Our design goal is to make the system adaptable to any irrigation system, so locally produced inputs can fit into the equipment growers already use. A key part of pilot work is learning how well it integrates with existing setups and what connections or adjustments would make that integration better. We also want to explore compatible spraying methods. Reviewing the delivery equipment, filtration, and application process together helps identify a practical setup; compatibility still needs to be confirmed before use.

How long does production take?

The current planning baseline is approximately two weeks for a production cycle. That gives us a starting point for discussing scheduling, rather than a final operating specification. Biological measurements are needed to establish the useful endpoint and the handling window after production. One purpose of pilot planning is to connect those findings with the application schedule, so production and use can be coordinated in a practical way.

Which crops or sites are you interested in?

We welcome conversations with growers, nurseries, and agricultural partners interested in exploring local biological production. A useful starting point is what you grow, where you operate, and the application you have in mind. That helps identify a focused question we could investigate together and the resources needed to do it well. You do not need a complete trial proposal to start the conversation.

What would participating in a pilot involve?

A useful pilot starts with a question worth answering. That might concern application fit, the work involved in local production, or how to compare a treatment with your usual practice. From there, we would discuss the site, equipment, timing, responsibilities, and observations needed. These details would be agreed before participation so everyone understands what the trial involves and what it is intended to establish. Your practical feedback would help shape the next stage of development.

What does a pilot cost?

An application trial and a local production pilot involve different resources. Equipment, inputs, setup, testing, and support all affect the scope and cost. We have not established a standard public pilot price. The first step is to understand the proposed work, then clarify the costs and responsibilities before you decide whether to participate. Starting that discussion does not require you to arrive with a finished budget or equipment plan.

What would a pilot help us understand?

A useful pilot should answer questions that matter in practice. Those could include plant establishment, root measurements, application practicality, operator effort, or the cost of producing and using the output. We would define the comparison and observations in advance so findings are easier to interpret. Good Growth is still building its evidence: the purpose is to learn where the system provides useful value and what needs improvement, rather than promise a particular crop response.

How are you approaching output quality?

Quality is central to making local production useful. The development plan includes assessing microbial identity, viability, contamination, and consistency between batches. Those measurements help determine what the system produces and how handling and application affect it. The methods and acceptance criteria still need to be established as part of that work. We want future operating guidance to be grounded in measured output, rather than relying on appearance or visible activity alone.
